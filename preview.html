Modals for Rules and Advice

Lesson 4A — 25 questions

Gap Fill

You ___ show your passport at the airport.

Answer: must

Must = no choice. Often used for laws, rules and very strong personal obligations.

I ___ get up early to catch my flight tomorrow.

Answer: need to

In the positive, must / have to / need to often mean the same thing — something is necessary.

You ___ finish that report today. Tomorrow will be fine.

Answer: don't have to

Don't have to / needn't / don't need to = optional, not compulsory. Compare with mustn't = forbidden.

You ___ use your phone during the exam.

Answer: mustn't

Mustn't = forbidden/not allowed. Don't confuse it with don't have to (= not necessary but allowed).

You ___ come to work in smart clothes.

Answer: should

Should = good idea / advice, not a rule. Compare: must (obligation) vs should (advice).

We ___ leave early yesterday because of the traffic.

Answer: had to

Must (present/future) → had to (past). There is no past tense of 'must' itself.

Error Spot

He must finishes the report by Friday.

Correction: finish

'Must' is followed by the bare infinitive: 'must finish', never 'must finishes'.

She have to leave early today.

Correction: has

Third person singular subjects need 'has to', not 'have to': 'She has to leave early today.'

You should arrive late to meetings.

Correction: shouldn't

Arriving late to meetings is bad advice, so the sentence needs the negative form: 'You shouldn't arrive late.'

You mustn't bring a laptop to the meeting if you don't want to.

Correction: needn't

The phrase 'if you don't want to' shows this is optional, not forbidden, so 'needn't' is correct.

You mustn't used your mobile phone during the flight.

Correction: use

'Mustn't' needs the base form: 'mustn't use', not 'mustn't used'.

We must leave early yesterday morning.

Correction: had to

'Yesterday morning' is in the past, so the sentence needs 'had to': 'We had to leave early yesterday morning.'

Multiple Choice

Which sentence describes something required, not just a suggestion?

Answer: You must wear a seatbelt in the car.

Must = a rule with no choice. Should/could/might = advice or suggestions, much weaker.

Which sentence expresses a prohibition?

Answer: You mustn't use your mobile phone on the plane.

Mustn't = forbidden. Don't have to / needn't = not necessary but still allowed.

Which sentence correctly expresses this idea: 'it isn't necessary, but you can if you want'?

Answer: You don't have to come to the meeting.

Don't have to = optional, your choice. Mustn't = forbidden — the opposite meaning.

Which sentence correctly continues the advice: 'Try eating fruit instead of sweet snacks.'?

Answer: You shouldn't eat lots of sweet snacks.

Friendly advice against something = shouldn't, not mustn't or don't have to.

Which sentence correctly talks about a PAST obligation?

Answer: We had to leave early yesterday because of the storm.

Must (present/future) → had to (past). Must never combines with 'to' or a past time word.

Which sentence is grammatically correct?

Answer: You should ask Helen for some advice.

Must and should are followed by the bare infinitive — never 'to', never -ing.

Which sentence correctly gives advice, not a rule or a statement about necessity?

Answer: You shouldn't stay this late every night.

Shouldn't = bad idea. Mustn't = forbidden. Don't have to/needn't = not necessary. Three different meanings.

Word Transform

You ___ (wear) a seatbelt every time you drive.

Answer: must wear

Must + base verb, no 'to': you must wear, not you must to wear.

We ___ (pay) tax on the money we earn.

Answer: have to pay

Have to + base verb: we have to pay, she has to pay.

You ___ (finish) that report today — tomorrow will be fine.

Answer: don't have to finish

Don't have to = optional. Compare with mustn't = forbidden.

You ___ (use) your phone during the exam.

Answer: mustn't use

Mustn't = not allowed. Never confuse it with don't have to (= not necessary but allowed).

You ___ (arrive) late to meetings — it looks unprofessional.

Answer: shouldn't arrive

Shouldn't = bad idea/advice, weaker than mustn't (which means forbidden).

We ___ (leave) early yesterday because of the traffic.

Answer: had to leave

Must (present/future) → had to (past). There's no such thing as 'musted'.
